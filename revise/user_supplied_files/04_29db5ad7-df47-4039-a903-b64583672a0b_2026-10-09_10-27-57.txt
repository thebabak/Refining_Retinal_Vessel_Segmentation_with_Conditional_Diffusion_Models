\abstract{
Retinal vessel segmentation supports quantitative vascular analysis but remains
difficult in low-contrast regions and along thin branches, where a single
feed-forward prediction cannot revise itself. We investigate whether a dedicated
diffusion stage can refine coarse probability maps produced by a lightweight
U-Net with reverse attention (LU-Net+RA). A frozen mask autoencoder maps each
coarse prediction to a feature grid with fourfold spatial downsampling; a
128-dimensional global RGB image embedding conditions the denoising network; and
a frozen decoder reconstructs the refined map after Denoising Diffusion Implicit
Model (DDIM) sampling. In contrast to refiners that diffuse an encoded
ground-truth target, the module applies continuous Gaussian diffusion to the
encoded coarse prediction, adding only 502{,}401 learned parameters. With 50
DDIM transitions, the method yielded Dice point estimates of 0.835, 0.835, and
0.793 on the specified CHASE-DB1, DRIVE, and HRF partitions; at this setting
the same-protocol LU-Net+RA baseline achieved a mean Dice of 0.8001 on the
same eight CHASE-DB1 test images, compared with 0.8350 after refinement,
an absolute increase of 0.0349 (3.49 percentage points). Comparisons with
published results on DRIVE and HRF remain contextual. On CHASE-DB1, Dice rose from 0.814 to 0.836 as
transitions increased from 10 to 100, characterising an adjustable accuracy--cost
trade-off. The results present a focused, parameter-efficient refinement design;
per-image paired results and repeated training runs remain necessary to assess
statistical significance and robustness. A separate new DRIVE baseline evaluation yielded Dice of 0.7879; this
standalone result does not establish the effect of refinement.}

\section{Introduction}

Retinal vessel segmentation supports quantitative analysis of vascular morphology in fundus images and may assist research on ocular and systemic disease. Convolutional neural network (CNN)-based methods have become a common approach to this task, including architectures such as U-Net and its variants, volumetric models such as V-Net~\cite{Milletari2016}, and context-aware designs such as CE-Net~\cite{Gu2019CENet}. Despite these advances, several technical challenges remain.

A primary difficulty is that vessel pixels occupy a relatively small part of a fundus image, producing substantial class imbalance. Under this imbalance, standard pixel-wise losses can underemphasise small structures, motivating alternatives such as focal loss~\cite{Lin2017}. Thin vessels and boundaries may also have low contrast, increasing the risk of missed or over-smoothed predictions. In addition, iterative refinement introduces a practical trade-off between predictive performance and computational cost.

Recent work has addressed some of these issues with lightweight architectures such as LU-Net with reverse attention (LU-Net+RA), which combines depth-wise separable convolutions with an attention mechanism intended to focus computation on missed regions~\cite{Hernandez2025}. Because a single feed-forward prediction cannot revise itself, an explicit post-processing model offers a mechanism for correcting missed vessel pixels and uncertain boundaries while retaining the coarse predictor as a fixed first stage. We therefore study segmentation refinement: a conditional diffusion module receives an LU-Net+RA probability map and the corresponding fundus image, then produces a revised vessel-probability map.

This work introduces a conditional feature-space diffusion model as a dedicated refinement stage. Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) provide an iterative formulation for generation and restoration~\cite{Ho2020,Dhariwal2021}, whereas latent diffusion performs the updates in an autoencoder representation~\cite{Rombach2022}. The representation used here is spatially downsampled but is not compressed in scalar count; we therefore use \emph{feature space} when describing it. The study reports segmentation results on three retinal datasets, compares them cautiously with a single, explicitly identified configuration from the published LU-Net+RA study, and analyses the effect of Denoising Diffusion Implicit Model (DDIM) transition count on refinement cost. Repeated sampling is used only to visualise pixel-wise output variability and is not presented as calibrated predictive uncertainty.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Retinal Vessel Segmentation}

Early retinal vessel segmentation methods relied on hand-crafted features, such as Gabor filters and matched filtering, combined with thresholding or classical classifiers~\cite{Soares2006, Mendonca2006}. While effective in controlled settings, these approaches lack robustness to variations in illumination and vessel appearance.

With the advent of deep learning, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), particularly U-Net~\cite{Ronneberger2015}, became the dominant paradigm for biomedical image segmentation, achieving strong performance on benchmark datasets such as DRIVE~\cite{Staal2004}. Subsequent work has focused on improving architectural design, including nested structures (UNet++~\cite{Zhou2018UNetpp}), attention mechanisms~\cite{Oktay2018AttUNet, Schlemper2019AG}, and recurrent or residual connections~\cite{Alom2018R2UNet}, as well as incorporating vessel-specific priors such as connectivity constraints and boundary information~\cite{Fu2016DeepVessel, Liskowski2016, Shin2019Connectivity}.

More recently, lightweight architectures such as LU-Net+RA~\cite{Hernandez2025} have aimed to balance accuracy and efficiency by leveraging depth-wise separable convolutions and reverse attention. Thin-vessel recovery and structural continuity remain relevant evaluation targets, particularly in low-contrast regions. These targets motivate testing whether a separate refinement strategy changes coarse predictions beneficially under a controlled protocol.

\subsection{Diffusion Models}

Diffusion models learn to reverse a progressive noise process~\cite{Ho2020}. DDIM permits deterministic transitions along a reduced timestep sequence~\cite{Song2021}, while latent diffusion performs these updates in an autoencoder representation~\cite{Rombach2022}.

Diffusion models have also been investigated for medical-image segmentation. Wolleb et al.~\cite{Wolleb2022} generated implicit segmentation ensembles, while SegDiff and MedSegDiff adapted conditional diffusion to general and medical segmentation tasks~\cite{SegDiff,MedSegDiff}. Pre-segmentation diffusion sampling starts the reverse process from a noised preliminary mask rather than pure Gaussian noise~\cite{PDDiffusion}, which is conceptually close to the initialisation used here. A broader survey is provided by Kazerouni et al.~\cite{Kazerouni2023}.

Recent work is more directly related to coarse-to-fine refinement. HiDiffSeg combines a coarse vessel estimate, vessel skeleton, and fundus-image guidance in a hierarchical diffusion model for retinal vessel segmentation~\cite{HiDiffSeg}. RefineCatDiff uses a coarse probability map as prior knowledge in a categorical diffusion process for medical-image segmentation~\cite{RefineCatDiff}. Li et al.~\cite{DiffusionFMD} instead introduce a plug-and-play diffusion denoising module for end-to-end feature-map refinement. The present method differs in four respects: it applies continuous Gaussian diffusion to the encoded LU-Net+RA probability map; freezes a mask autoencoder during refinement training; conditions the denoiser with a global RGB embedding; and adds 502,401 learned parameters as a post-processing stage. These distinctions define the architectural contribution, but they do not establish performance superiority because no controlled comparison with these methods is available.

Unlike formulations that diffuse an encoded ground-truth target, the proposed training process adds noise to the encoded coarse mask and uses a decoded Dice term to encourage agreement with the reference mask. This choice makes the method a Dice-regularised denoiser of the coarse prediction rather than a conventional target-diffusion model. A deterministic learned-refiner control and target-diffusion ablation would be required to isolate the benefit of the diffusion formulation.

\subsection{Attention Mechanisms in Medical Imaging}

Attention mechanisms are widely used in medical imaging to enhance feature representation by focusing on relevant regions. Channel and spatial attention modules, such as SE-Net~\cite{Hu2018} and CBAM~\cite{Woo2018}, improve performance by reweighting feature responses. In contrast, reverse attention~\cite{Hernandez2025} explicitly emphasises regions that are likely to be misclassified, enabling the model to focus on difficult structures.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Retinal vessel segmentation is formulated as a dense prediction task in which each pixel of a fundus image is classified as vessel or background. Let $\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times512\times512}$ denote an RGB fundus image, and let $\mathbf{M}_{gt}\in\{0,1\}^{1\times512\times512}$ denote the corresponding ground-truth mask. The objective is to learn a mapping $\mathcal{F}:\mathbf{I}\mapsto \mathbf{M}^*$, where $\mathbf{M}^*\in[0,1]^{1\times512\times512}$ is the predicted vessel probability map (thresholded to obtain a binary mask during evaluation).

Instead of a single-stage prediction, we adopt a coarse-to-fine framework. A lightweight segmentation model first produces a coarse estimate $\mathbf{M}_0\in[0,1]^{1\times512\times512}$, which captures the main vessel structure but may contain discontinuities, missing thin vessels, and blurred boundaries.

To refine this estimate, $\mathbf{M}_0$ is mapped to a feature-space representation $\mathbf{z}_0=\mathrm{Encode}(\mathbf{M}_0)$, where $\mathrm{Encode}(\cdot)$ denotes a mask encoder and $\mathbf{z}_0\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$ is a spatially downsampled learned grid. A conditional diffusion model $\mathrm{Refine}_{\theta}(\cdot)$ then updates $\mathbf{z}_0$ iteratively while conditioning on the input image $\mathbf{I}$. The refined feature grid is mapped back to pixel space using a decoder $\mathrm{Decode}(\cdot)$, yielding:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}^* = \mathrm{Decode}\big(\mathrm{Refine}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_0, \mathbf{I})\big).
\end{equation}

This formulation separates coarse prediction from iterative refinement and provides a tunable additional-computation stage.

\subsection{Baseline: Lightweight U-Net with Reverse Attention}

For coarse segmentation, we use LU-Net+RA~\cite{Hernandez2025}, a lightweight encoder--decoder with depth-wise separable convolutions. Its key component is reverse attention (RA), which emphasises uncertain regions. Given a feature map $\mathbf{F}$ and attention logits $\mathbf{A}$, the RA output is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}^{\text{RA}} = \mathbf{F}\otimes \left(1-\sigma(\mathbf{A})\right),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function and $\otimes$ denotes element-wise multiplication. Here, $\sigma(\mathbf{A})$ highlights predicted vessel regions, while its complement focuses the model on hard or misclassified areas. The resulting reverse-attention-modulated features are decoded to produce the coarse prediction $\mathbf{M}_0$ used by the diffusion refinement stage.

Importantly, this RA mechanism is used only within the LU-Net+RA backbone. The diffusion refiner itself does not receive a separate RA map tensor as an additional input. Instead, the diffusion stage is conditioned on the coarse mask latent $\mathbf{z}_0=\mathrm{Encode}(\mathbf{M}_0)$ and the image embedding $\mathbf{c}=\mathrm{ImageEncoder}(\mathbf{I}_{RGB})$. In other words, reverse-attention guidance is implemented implicitly via the RA-enhanced coarse prediction $\mathbf{M}_0$, rather than as an explicit extra conditioning channel in the diffusion U-Net.

\subsection{Proposed Conditional Feature-Space Diffusion Refiner}

To refine the coarse segmentation, we operate on a learned multi-channel feature grid with lower spatial resolution than the input mask. The proposed refinement framework consists of a mask autoencoder, an image encoder for conditioning, and a conditional diffusion model defined in this learned representation space.

\subsubsection{Mask Autoencoder}

We use a lightweight convolutional autoencoder that maps a vessel mask $\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times512\times512}$ to a feature grid $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{64\times128\times128}$, corresponding to a $4\times$ reduction along each spatial dimension. The input mask contains $1\times512\times512=262{,}144$ scalar values, whereas the multi-channel grid contains $64\times128\times128=1{,}048{,}576$ values. It is therefore spatially downsampled but not compressed in scalar count. The architecture is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Encoder: Conv($1\rightarrow32$, $3\times3$, stride 2, padding 1) + ReLU $\rightarrow$ Conv($32\rightarrow64$, $3\times3$, stride 2, padding 1) + ReLU $\rightarrow$ Conv($64\rightarrow64$, $1\times1$, stride 1).
  \item Decoder: two $4\times4$ transposed convolutions ($64\rightarrow64$ and $64\rightarrow32$; stride 2, padding 1), each followed by ReLU, then a $1\times1$ convolution ($32\rightarrow1$) and sigmoid activation.
\end{itemize}

Therefore, the spatial path is $512\times512\rightarrow256\times256\rightarrow128\times128$ in the encoder and $128\times128\rightarrow256\times256\rightarrow512\times512$ in the decoder, while channel progression is $1\rightarrow32\rightarrow64\rightarrow64$ (encoder) and $64\rightarrow64\rightarrow32\rightarrow1$ (decoder). The autoencoder has approximately $0.121$M trainable parameters in total (about $22{,}976$ in the encoder and $98{,}433$ in the decoder).

For an input ground-truth mask $\mathbf{M}_{gt}$, the autoencoder reconstruction is
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}=D_m\!\left(E_m(\mathbf{M}_{gt})\right).
\end{equation}
The reconstruction term is the mean squared error over the $N=512\times512$ pixels,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(\tilde{\mathbf{M}},\mathbf{M}_{gt})=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}
\left(\tilde{M}_{p}-M_{gt,p}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
For any predicted probability map $\mathbf{P}$ and binary target $\mathbf{G}$, we use the soft Dice loss
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{G})=
1-\frac{2\sum_{p=1}^{N}P_pG_p}
{\sum_{p=1}^{N}P_p+\sum_{p=1}^{N}G_p}.
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the autoencoder objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{AE}} =
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(\tilde{\mathbf{M}},\mathbf{M}_{gt})+
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}(\tilde{\mathbf{M}},\mathbf{M}_{gt}).
\end{equation}
All sums are evaluated over the complete resized mask. The autoencoder is pretrained on ground-truth masks only, without additional mask augmentation. It is therefore trained on manually annotated binary masks rather than coarse LU-Net+RA probability maps. After pretraining, its parameters are frozen and used to encode $\mathbf{M}_0$. The distribution shift between binary training masks and soft coarse probability maps is analysed as a limitation in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{Image Conditioning}

To incorporate global contextual information, the diffusion refinement stage uses a custom lightweight CNN image encoder (not ResNet/MobileNet) fed by the resized and normalised RGB fundus image. Specifically, the encoder receives $\mathbf{I}_{RGB}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times512\times512}$ and maps it to a compact feature vector $\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c}=\mathrm{ImageEncoder}(\mathbf{I}_{RGB}).
\end{equation}

Its layer stack is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Conv($3\rightarrow32$, $3\times3$, stride 2, padding 1) + ReLU,
  \item Conv($32\rightarrow64$, $3\times3$, stride 2, padding 1) + ReLU,
  \item Adaptive average pooling to $1\times1$,
  \item Flatten,
  \item Linear($64\rightarrow128$) to produce the final embedding $\mathbf{c}$.
\end{itemize}

Thus, the feature map is downsampled as $512\times512\rightarrow256\times256\rightarrow128\times128\rightarrow1\times1$, then projected to a 128-dimensional conditioning vector. This image encoder has approximately $0.028$M trainable parameters ($27{,}712$ parameters) and is trained from scratch during diffusion-refiner training (no external pretrained weights are used).

This RGB conditioning branch is separate from the preprocessing used by the coarse LU-Net+RA segmentation backbone. The backbone follows the vessel-enhancement preprocessing pipeline, including green-channel extraction and contrast enhancement, to produce the coarse vessel probability map $\mathbf{M}_0$. In contrast, the diffusion image encoder is not given a green-channel-only image or a CLAHE-enhanced single-channel input; it receives the three-channel RGB fundus image after resizing and normalisation. This design allows the refiner to exploit colour and illumination cues while the diffusion process operates on the encoded coarse mask.
\subsubsection{Feature-Space Diffusion Process}

The refinement is formulated as a diffusion process in the encoded feature space. Starting from the encoded coarse mask $\mathbf{z}_0=\mathrm{Encode}(\mathbf{M}_0)$, noise is progressively added according to a cosine schedule~\cite{Nichol2021}:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\alpha}_t=\frac{\cos^2\!\left(\frac{t/T+0.008}{1.008}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\right)}{\cos^2\!\left(\frac{0.008}{1.008}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\right)},
\end{equation}
where $t\in\{0,\dots,T\}$ denotes the diffusion step and $\bar{\alpha}_t$ is the normalised cumulative signal-retention schedule, with $\bar{\alpha}_0=1$. For $t\in\{1,\dots,T\}$, the corresponding per-step quantities are $\alpha_t=\bar{\alpha}_t/\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}$ and $\beta_t=1-\alpha_t$. We use a diffusion horizon of $T=1000$ and sample training timesteps uniformly from $\{1,\dots,T-1\}$. During inference, DDIM sampling retains a shorter timestep sequence, typically containing 20 or 50 reverse transitions, as analysed in Table~\ref{tab:ddim_steps}. The forward process generates noisy feature variables:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_t=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\mathbf{z}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, 
\quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ is Gaussian noise.

A conditional diffusion U-Net is then trained to reverse this process by predicting the noise component $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,t,\mathbf{c})$, conditioned on both the noisy feature tensor $\mathbf{z}_t$ and the image embedding $\mathbf{c}$.


\subsubsection{Feature-Conditioning Injection}

To inject image information into the diffusion model, the 128-dimensional image embedding $\mathbf{c}$ is projected by learned conditioning layers and added to intermediate residual features of the diffusion U-Net. The timestep embedding is injected through the same residual-block conditioning mechanism. This feature-wise conditioning supplies global fundus context throughout denoising without introducing a separate spatial cross-attention map. Reverse attention remains confined to the LU-Net+RA backbone that produces the coarse mask $\mathbf{M}_0$; no separate RA tensor is injected into the diffusion U-Net.

\subsubsection{Training Objective}

The model is trained to predict the noise added during the forward diffusion process while incorporating segmentation-aware constraints. Let $N_z$ be the number of elements in a feature tensor. The mean DDPM noise-prediction loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDPM}}=
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}_0,t,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\left[
\frac{1}{N_z}\left\|
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,t,\mathbf{c})-
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
\right\|_2^2
\right],
\end{equation}
with $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$ denoting Gaussian noise and $\mathbf{z}_t$ the noisy feature tensor obtained from $\mathbf{z}_0$ at timestep $t$.
The noise prediction is then used to estimate the clean feature tensor corresponding to the sampled timestep:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0^{(t)}=
\frac{\mathbf{z}_t-
\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t,t,\mathbf{c})}
{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}.
\end{equation}
Because the pretrained mask decoder is frozen and includes its output sigmoid, the associated vessel-probability map is
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_t=D_m\!\left(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0^{(t)}\right).
\end{equation}
The segmentation-aware term is therefore $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}(\hat{\mathbf{M}}_t,\mathbf{M}_{gt})$, using the pixel-space definition given above, and the complete diffusion-refiner objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DDPM}}+
\lambda_{\text{dice}}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}(\hat{\mathbf{M}}_t,\mathbf{M}_{gt}).
\end{equation}
In the implementation evaluated here, $\lambda_{\text{dice}}=0.5$; no separate Sobel edge-loss term is applied.

This objective is Dice-regularised denoising of the encoded coarse mask. The DDPM term is defined with respect to Gaussian noise added to $E_m(\mathbf{M}_0)$ and therefore favours recovery of the coarse feature grid, whereas the decoded Dice term encourages agreement with the ground-truth vessel mask. The method is not formulated as unconditional generation of $E_m(\mathbf{M}_{gt})$. This distinction is important when comparing it with formulations that diffuse the ground-truth target and provide the coarse mask as a separate condition.

The models are trained in two stages. The mask autoencoder is pretrained for 50 epochs using AdamW with a learning rate of $1\times10^{-4}$ on ground-truth vessel masks only. Its weights are then frozen, and the diffusion refiner is trained for 100 epochs using AdamW at $1\times10^{-4}$ with cosine annealing over the full training schedule. During diffusion training, only the image encoder, conditioning multilayer perceptron (MLP), and diffusion U-Net are updated. A classifier-free-guidance conditioning dropout of 10\% is applied during training. No additional image or mask augmentation is used. Images and masks are resized to $512\times512$, and LU-Net+RA probability maps are loaded as the coarse conditions. No explicit field-of-view loss mask or crop is applied during refiner training.

\subsubsection{DDIM Sampling}

For inference, we employ DDIM sampling~\cite{Song2021} to generate a refined feature representation from the initial code $\mathbf{z}_0=\mathrm{Encode}(\mathbf{M}_0)$. Instead of starting from pure noise, the sampling process is initialised from a noisy version of $\mathbf{z}_0$, preserving an explicit connection to the coarse prediction throughout the refinement trajectory.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{Feature-space refinement sampling procedure.}
\label{alg:latent_refinement}
\centering
\begin{minipage}{0.96\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Inputs:} coarse mask $\mathbf{M}_0$; RGB image $\mathbf{I}_{RGB}$; encoders $E_m,E_I$; decoder $D_m$; noise network $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$; schedule $\{\bar{\alpha}_t\}_{t=0}^{T}$; refinement strength $\rho$; DDIM transitions $S$; guidance scale $s$; sample count $K$.\par
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item Encode $\mathbf{z}_0=E_m(\mathbf{M}_0)$ and $\mathbf{c}=E_I(\mathbf{I}_{RGB})$; set the null condition $\mathbf{c}_{\emptyset}=\mathbf{0}$.
  \item Set $t_s=\lfloor\rho T\rfloor$ and $t_i=\lfloor t_s(S-i)/S\rfloor$ for $i=0,\ldots,S$, so $t_0=t_s$ and $t_S=0$.
  \item For each sample $k=1,\ldots,K$, draw $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})$ and initialise $\mathbf{z}^{(k)}_{t_0}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t_0}}\mathbf{z}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t_0}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{(k)}$.
  \item For $i=0,\ldots,S-1$, evaluate $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{c}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{z}^{(k)}_{t_i},t_i,\mathbf{c})$ and $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{u}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{z}^{(k)}_{t_i},t_i,\mathbf{c}_{\emptyset})$, then form $\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{u}+s(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{c}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{u})$.
  \item Estimate $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0=(\mathbf{z}^{(k)}_{t_i}-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t_i}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})/\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t_i}}$ and update $\mathbf{z}^{(k)}_{t_{i+1}}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t_{i+1}}}\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t_{i+1}}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$.
  \item Decode $\tilde{\mathbf{M}}^{(k)}=D_m(\mathbf{z}^{(k)}_{t_S})$ and return $\mathbf{M}^*=K^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}^{(k)}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
\end{table}

The starting timestep $t_s$ controls refinement strength: a larger value adds more noise to the encoded coarse mask and permits a stronger correction, whereas a smaller value preserves more of the LU-Net+RA prediction. The $S+1$ retained timesteps are uniformly spaced between $t_s$ and zero, producing exactly $S$ reverse transitions. At every reverse step, the diffusion U-Net is evaluated with both the image condition and a null condition. Their predictions are combined using classifier-free guidance, after which the deterministic DDIM update ($\eta_{\mathrm{DDIM}}=0$) maps the current feature tensor to the next timestep. Because the mask decoder already ends with a sigmoid activation, the procedure in Table~\ref{alg:latent_refinement} does not apply a second sigmoid. We use $K=1$ for the primary segmentation results. Although each reverse trajectory is deterministic after its initial noise sample is fixed, the complete procedure remains stochastic with respect to that initial sample. The qualitative variability analysis uses multiple independent initial-noise samples and reports the pixel-wise variance of their decoded maps.

Two preprocessing streams are used. The LU-Net+RA backbone follows the preprocessing pipeline of Hernandez-Gutierrez et al.~\cite{Hernandez2025}: green-channel extraction, contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalisation (CLAHE), inverse gamma correction, resizing to $512\times512$, and normalisation to $[0,1]$. The 20- and 50-transition variants use the same stated partitions and coarse inputs.

\subsection{Training and Implementation Details}

Training is sequential. LU-Net+RA is trained first and then frozen. Its stored probability maps condition the refiner, whose training does not update the backbone. The coarse maps used to train the refiner were generated from images that had also been used to fit LU-Net+RA; they are therefore in-sample rather than out-of-fold predictions. Freezing prevents joint adaptation between the stages, but it does not remove the possible train-test shift between in-sample coarse predictions and predictions for unseen images.

The mask autoencoder is pretrained for 50 epochs on the corresponding training masks and then frozen. The image encoder, conditioning layers, and diffusion U-Net are trained for 100 epochs using AdamW, an initial learning rate of $10^{-4}$, and cosine annealing. Conditioning dropout is 10\%, and no additional geometric, image, or mask augmentation is applied. Images, masks, and coarse probability maps are resized deterministically to $512\times512$. The original files do not specify interpolation kernels, aspect-ratio handling, training batch size, AdamW weight decay, checkpoint selection, or a validation partition.

Refinement-stage timing uses a batch size of 1 on one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti SUPER graphics processing unit, evaluation mode, and disabled gradient computation. The timing includes mask encoding, DDIM refinement, and mask decoding but excludes the LU-Net+RA forward pass. Mixed-precision inference was not used. Because the warm-up count, number of repetitions, and timing dispersion are unavailable, all runtime values are reported as approximate rather than as benchmark estimates.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Vessel pixels are treated as the positive class. Let TP, TN, FP, and FN denote true-positive, true-negative, false-positive, and false-negative pixel counts, respectively. The metrics appearing in the result tables are
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Dice} &= \frac{2\mathrm{TP}}{2\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}}, \\
\mathrm{Accuracy} &= \frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}}
{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}}, \\
\mathrm{Sensitivity} &= \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}, \\
\mathrm{Specificity} &= \frac{\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}}.
\end{align}
The original reported metrics were restricted to a field-of-view (FOV) mask. Training losses, by contrast, were evaluated over the full resized canvas, with pixels outside the retinal field represented as background. For the original experiments, the exact prediction threshold, threshold-selection rule, dataset-specific FOV-mask provenance, and the aggregation rules for DRIVE and HRF are unavailable. The separate new DRIVE experiment below documents its threshold and aggregation rule. CHASE-DB1 Dice is reported as the mean across the eight test images for both the coarse baseline and refined predictions. The tables must therefore be interpreted as single-run point estimates rather than independently reproducible or inferential statistics.

\subsection{Quantitative Segmentation Results}

Table~\ref{tab:segmentation_results} reports the results produced by the diffusion-refinement configurations and the same-protocol CHASE-DB1 coarse baseline. On the same eight CHASE-DB1 test images, LU-Net+RA achieved a mean Dice of 0.8001, whereas the diffusion-refined method with 50 DDIM transitions achieved 0.8350 under the same evaluation protocol. This corresponds to an absolute increase of 0.0349 (3.49 percentage points). Statistical significance is not established from these mean scores alone. \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The proposed refiner is most closely related to coarse-to-fine diffusion systems rather than to generic post-processing. HiDiffSeg uses hierarchical vessel and skeleton guidance for retinal segmentation~\cite{HiDiffSeg}; RefineCatDiff incorporates a coarse probability map as prior knowledge in categorical diffusion~\cite{RefineCatDiff}; and the diffusion feature-map denoising module of Li et al.~\cite{DiffusionFMD} is trained end-to-end within existing segmenters. The present design instead performs continuous Gaussian diffusion on an encoded LU-Net+RA probability map, freezes the mask autoencoder, and uses a global RGB condition. These differences motivate the method, but a controlled benchmark is required before claiming an advantage over the related alternatives.

The 50-transition configuration produced the same reported Dice (0.835) on CHASE-DB1 and DRIVE and a lower value (0.793) on HRF. HRF includes healthy, diabetic-retinopathy, and glaucomatous images at substantially higher native resolution, so pathology and resampling are plausible contributors to the lower estimate; this interpretation was not tested experimentally. On DRIVE, the decrease in sensitivity from 0.835 at 20 transitions to 0.825 at 50 transitions, despite higher Dice, suggests a shift in the balance between false positives and false negatives. Per-image confusion counts and threshold sweeps are needed to determine the source of that change. The CHASE-DB1 transition-count series shows diminishing numerical gains beyond 50 transitions, but each setting is represented by only one initial-noise realisation.

The method adds an adjustable computational stage. The reported 20- and 50-transition settings require approximately 40 and 100 ms for refinement alone, and every classifier-free-guidance transition evaluates the diffusion U-Net twice. The coarse LU-Net+RA forward pass is excluded, so the values do not describe complete-pipeline latency or establish real-time performance.

\subsection{Limitations and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:limitations}

The same-protocol CHASE-DB1 mean scores favour refinement: Dice increased from 0.8001 for LU-Net+RA to 0.8350 after 50 DDIM transitions on the same eight test images. However, per-image paired results, confidence intervals, and repeated training runs remain necessary to assess statistical significance and robustness. For DRIVE, Table~\ref{tab:drive_baseline_new} reports a separate baseline evaluation, but this standalone score does not establish the effect of refinement or validate the earlier DRIVE estimate of 0.835. Same-case coarse metrics remain unavailable for HRF. Table~\ref{tab:published_context} uses a clearly identified published configuration, but its cases and evaluation pipeline differ from those of Table~\ref{tab:segmentation_results}; those cross-study differences therefore cannot be attributed to diffusion.

Several implementation and evaluation details are unavailable: the numerical refinement strength $\rho$; the evaluation seed; the sample count and display scale for Figure~\ref{fig:sampling_variability}; exact split identities and subject-level separation; quantitative observer selection; interpolation and aspect-ratio handling; FOV-mask provenance; threshold rules and DRIVE/HRF aggregation rules; validation and checkpoint selection; training batch size and AdamW weight decay; the layer-by-layer diffusion U-Net specification; and runtime warm-up and repetition counts. These omissions prevent exact reproduction. The reported loss equations should also be checked against the executable implementation for smoothing and reduction conventions before code release.

The refiner is trained on in-sample backbone predictions rather than out-of-fold predictions, and its mask autoencoder is pretrained on binary annotations but later encodes soft probability maps. Both choices can create a train-test input shift. The global 128-dimensional image vector also discards spatial conditioning information. Finally, the $64\times128\times128$ feature grid is spatially coarser than the input mask but contains more scalar values, so the method should not be described as a compressed latent bottleneck.

The study reports neither component ablations nor a deterministic learned-refiner control. The qualitative cases were selected for visible effects, and no centreline, connectivity, boundary, or calibration metric was computed. Future experiments should use out-of-fold coarse predictions, paired multi-seed evaluation, topology-aware metrics, unbiased qualitative sampling, calibrated variability analysis, and controlled comparisons with recent coarse-to-fine diffusion refiners.

%%%%==============================================================


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study presents a 502,401-parameter conditional feature-space diffusion module for refining LU-Net+RA retinal vessel probability maps. With 50 DDIM transitions, the reported Dice point estimates were 0.835 on CHASE-DB1, 0.835 on DRIVE, and 0.793 on HRF. The CHASE-DB1 transition-count analysis ranged from 0.814 at 10 transitions to 0.836 at 100 transitions, illustrating an adjustable refinement-stage accuracy-cost trade-off. Repeated sampling additionally visualised output variability without being treated as calibrated uncertainty.

On the same eight CHASE-DB1 test images and under the same evaluation protocol, the LU-Net+RA baseline achieved a mean Dice of 0.8001 compared with 0.8350 after refinement, an absolute increase of 0.0349 (3.49 percentage points). This single-run comparison does not establish statistical significance or robustness. Per-image paired results, confidence intervals, repeated training runs, and a same-protocol HRF baseline remain necessary. The separate new DRIVE baseline yielded Dice of 0.7879, but a standalone baseline score does not verify improvement over the coarse predictor. The architecture provides a focused design for studying lightweight diffusion-based refinement of retinal vessel segmentations.
