1.	The most critical issue is the absence of paired comparison. The authors use published LU-Net+RA results as context, but they do not evaluate the coarse prediction and the refined prediction on the same test images under the same protocol. Without paired results, one cannot tell whether the Dice improvement comes from the diffusion refiner or from differences in dataset splits and evaluation procedures. The authors must add coarse metrics on the same split and report per-image Dice changes, means, and confidence intervals.
Thank you for this important comment. We have revised the manuscript to report paired coarse and refined predictions on the same test images. The authors confirm that both predictions used identical ground-truth masks, preprocessing, image resolution, FOV masks, binarisation thresholds, and metric calculation. The refiner received the same coarse predictions used to calculate the baseline metrics.
At 50 DDIM transitions, the paired results were:

We now provide per-image coarse and refined Dice scores and their paired differences. Confidence intervals were calculated using 50,000 crossed bootstrap resamples of training labels and test units, after averaging sampling realisations. CHASE-DB1 resampling retained both eyes together within each subject; HRF resampling was stratified by pathology.
These comparisons quantify the observed refinement change within the supplied paired evaluations. Published LU-Net+RA results are retained only as context. We interpret the intervals cautiously because of the small test sets, particularly the four CHASE-DB1 subjects, and the unverified patient independence of DRIVE and HRF images.

2.	All results are single-run point estimates. There are no repeated seeds, no standard deviations, and no statistical tests. The Dice change from 10 to 100 DDIM transitions is small, and a single run cannot establish a reliable trend. At least five repeated runs with different random seeds are needed, with means, standard deviations, and paired tests for key comparisons.
Thank you for this comment. We have expanded the manuscript to report results for five training-seed labels (42, 123, 2026, 3000, and 3001), with five sampling realisations per label, at 10, 20, 30, 50, and 100 DDIM transitions. Training-seed and sampling-seed variation are explicitly distinguished.
At 50 transitions, refined Dice across the five training-label means was:
Dataset	Mean Dice ± SD
CHASE-DB1	0.835000 ± 0.002910
DRIVE	0.835000 ± 0.008739
HRF	0.793000 ± 0.007189
We added two-sided paired t-tests and exact Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for baseline versus 50 transitions, 10 versus 50, and 50 versus 100, with separate Holm corrections across the nine comparisons for each test type. Baseline–refinement differences were significant in the run-level t-tests, but not in the adjusted exact signed-rank tests. Both results are reported.
Neither the 10-versus-50 nor the 50-versus-100 comparison was significant in either adjusted test. We therefore treat the transition-count trend cautiously and do not claim that additional transitions reliably improve Dice. Nonsignificance does not establish equivalence or confirm that the original 0.001 difference was caused by sampling variation.
The run-level analysis assumes that the supplied training-seed labels represent independent training executions; their execution records have not been independently verified. Thus, the numerical reporting has been expanded, while this verification remains a limitation.




3.	There is no controlled comparison with existing diffusion refinement methods. The paper mentions HiDiffSeg, RefineCatDiff, and the plug-and-play diffusion denoising module of Li et al., but does not compare against them under the same setting. If reproduction is not possible, the authors should at least explain why and include a deterministic learned refiner as a baseline to isolate the benefit of the diffusion formulation.




Thank you for this comment. We added a spatial deterministic U-Net refiner, trained with five independent seeds on each dataset, and report paired coarse-to-refiner results, standard deviations, and confidence intervals in Section 4.14. The supplied 50-step diffusion means are numerically higher than the spatial-refiner means on all three datasets. These scores originate from separate sources without matching original diffusion checkpoints and prediction records, so the comparison is descriptive and does not establish a controlled diffusion advantage. Same-protocol numerical comparisons with HiDiffSeg, RefineCatDiff, and Li et al. remain outstanding; we do not claim that architectural differences make their reproduction impossible.

4.	There are no component ablations. For example, there is no target-diffusion baseline, no comparison of conditioning injection methods, and no comparison between freezing and fine-tuning the mask autoencoder. Without these ablations, the design choices are not supported. I suggest at least four ablations, including a deterministic refiner, target diffusion, no image conditioning, and spatial conditioning instead of a global vector.
Thank you for this comment. We conducted a separate controlled component-ablation study on CHASE-DB1, DRIVE, and HRF, using five independently trained refiners per configuration. The study includes deterministic refinement, target diffusion, removal of image conditioning, spatial conditioning, additive versus FiLM injection, and frozen versus fine-tuned mask autoencoders.
Our original diffusion results have higher mean Dice than the newly evaluated deterministic and fine-tuned-autoencoder refiners across all three datasets. Within the new matched study, deterministic refinement and autoencoder fine-tuning outperform the reference configuration used in that component-ablation study. Conditioning changes produce small differences, while target diffusion gives mixed results with coarse initialization and performs poorly with Gaussian initialization. We have added these results and their limitations to the manuscript and do not claim that the original design is optimal.

5.	The dataset splits and evaluation protocol are unclear. Is the CHASE-DB1 20/8 split subject-level independent. Is the HRF 33/12 split stratified by pathology. There is no validation set and no checkpoint selection strategy. The evaluation threshold, the FOV mask provenance, and whether results are averaged per image or computed from pooled pixels are all unknown. These details must be provided.

Thank you for this comment. Examination of the executable rerun code establishes the protocol used for our separate experiments. CHASE-DB1 is divided by subject into 16 training, 4 validation, and 8 test images (8, 2, and 4 subjects), retaining both eyes of each subject in one partition. DRIVE uses its official 20-image test set, with 16 training and 4 validation images drawn from the training pool. HRF uses a pathology-stratified 27/6/12 training/validation/test split, with 9/2/4 images from each pathology category. Thus, the 20 and 33 non-test images include validation images in these experiments.
Predictions are binarised at probability >=0.5. Metrics are computed per image and averaged rather than calculated from pooled pixels. CHASE-DB1 is evaluated over the whole image; DRIVE uses the local dataset FOV GIF masks, and HRF uses the field-of-view bitmap in the local annotation JSON. Binary masks are resized using nearest-neighbour interpolation. The upstream conversion provenance of the HRF annotation files remains undocumented.
The coarse predictor is selected by validation mean Dice, with a maximum of 1000 epochs and early-stopping patience of 20. The newly trained component-ablation refiners use the predefined final checkpoint at epoch100, without test-based selection. These settings document the newly executed experiments. We cannot establish from these scripts alone that the original author-supplied diffusion means were produced using the same protocol, so we do not retrospectively assign these settings to the original results.


6.	The coarse predictions used to train the refiner are in-sample predictions from images that LU-Net+RA was trained on. This creates a train-test shift. The refiner should be trained on out-of-fold coarse predictions, or at least the impact should be discussed and tested.

Thank you for this comment. We acknowledge that the original refiner used in-sample coarse predictions. Section 4.16 now describes the separate executable out-of-fold protocol and also reports the author-supplied OOF summary table as a distinct descriptive source. In that supplied table, in-sample and OOF Dice are 0.835001 and 0.834745 on CHASE-DB1, 0.835000 and 0.834849 on DRIVE, and 0.793000 and 0.792758 on HRF. Differences calculated from unrounded values are -0.000256, -0.000151, and -0.000243, respectively. Execution provenance for this table remains unresolved: its in-sample records match the previously supplied adjusted component-ablation table. We therefore do not present these summaries as independently verified outcomes of the executable protocol. The small descriptive changes do not establish equivalence or rule out train-test distribution shift. No confidence interval or significance result from another experiment is assigned to these values.

7.	The term feature space is misleading. The encoded grid is spatially downsampled by a factor of four, but it contains about four times more scalar values than the input mask, so it is not a compressed representation. The authors should describe it as a spatially downsampled multi-channel representation and avoid terms like latent or compressed.

Thank you for this clarification. The input mask contains 512 × 512 = 262,144 scalar values, whereas the encoded grid contains 64 × 128 × 128 = 1,048,576, four times as many. It is therefore downsampled fourfold along each spatial axis, but is not compressed in total scalar count. We have revised the manuscript to describe it consistently as a “spatially downsampled multi-channel representation” and avoid terminology suggesting compression. Parameter efficiency refers to the model’s parameter count, rather than the size of this representation.

8.	The repeated-sampling variability analysis is only qualitative. The number of samples, display scale, and calibration metrics are missing. If this analysis is kept, it should be made quantitative, for example by relating variance to error or by providing reliability diagrams. Otherwise, the calibration implications should be removed.
Thank you for this comment. We have removed the qualitative repeated-sampling variance figure and its accompanying analysis because the original sample count and display-scale metadata could not be verified. We have also removed the associated statements from the introduction, contribution summary, and conclusion. The remaining repeated-seed segmentation results are reported separately and are not interpreted as evidence of calibrated predictive uncertainty.



9.	Many implementation details are missing, including the refinement strength rho, the evaluation seed, interpolation kernels, batch size, weight decay, warm-up, FOV mask provenance, and the layer-by-layer diffusion U-Net specification. The authors acknowledge this in the limitations, but the revised manuscript should provide as much as possible and release code and weights.
Thank you for this comment. We expanded the manuscript to document the executable protocol, including partitions, training and sampling seeds, interpolation, batch size, AdamW learning rate and weight decay, checkpoint selection, threshold, FOV-mask sources, and per-image aggregation. We provide a layer-by-layer denoiser specification and a timing protocol with five warm-up runs and ten timed repetitions per image and mode. The executable refiner uses a 1,000-timestep schedule, starts at timestep 99, and performs 50 DDIM transitions with eta = 0 and classifier-free guidance scale 1.5. These settings are distinguished from unresolved details of the original reported runs. Upstream HRF annotation conversion provenance remains undocumented. Implementation files and retained executable checkpoints are held locally but have not been publicly released or included in the current submission package. We therefore do not claim that the code-and-weights release request has been fulfilled; the original score-producing diffusion checkpoint records remain unavailable.
