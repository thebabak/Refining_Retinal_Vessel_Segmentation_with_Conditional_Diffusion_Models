1.	The reported improvement over LU-Net+RA cannot be verified quantitatively. For CHASE-DB1, the proposed method reports Dice = 0.835, compared with 0.7946 for the published LU-Net+RA result, a nominal gain of 0.0404. However, these values come from different test protocols. The paper should report the actual Dice of LU-Net+RA on the same 8 CHASE-DB1 test images before claiming improvement.

We thank the reviewer for this important observation. We agree that the published LU-Net+RA Dice score of 0.7946 was obtained under a different evaluation protocol and cannot establish the refinement effect in our experiment. Our LU-Net+RA baseline achieved a mean Dice of 0.8001 on the same eight CHASE-DB1 test images, using the same evaluation protocol as the diffusion-refined method. The refined method achieved 0.8350, corresponding to an absolute Dice increase of 0.0349 (3.49 percentage points). We will include this same-protocol baseline in the revised results table and base the improvement claim on this comparison. The published score of 0.7946 will be retained only as contextual information. We do not claim statistical significance from these mean scores alone.
2.	The DRIVE comparison is similarly difficult to interpret. The reported Dice is 0.835 versus 0.7871 for published LU-Net+RA, a difference of 0.0479, but the sensitivity of the proposed model (0.825) is substantially higher than the published LU-Net+RA sensitivity (0.7421). Because the evaluation protocols are different, this apparent improvement should not be interpreted as an effect size without same-image testing.
Thank you for this comment. The published LU-Net+RA values are retained solely as literature context. Our within-study baseline yielded Dice of 0.7879 and sensitivity of 0.8602, compared with 0.835 and 0.825 for the 50-transition refinement. Under the author-confirmed matching of test images and evaluation settings, the absolute Dice increase is 0.0471, while sensitivity decreases by 0.0352. We therefore do not claim improvement across all metrics. The original diffusion checkpoint and prediction records remain unavailable for independent verification of this matching.



3.	The HRF result is not consistently better. The proposed 20-step model obtains Dice = 0.762, which is actually 0.0136 lower than the published LU-Net+RA value of 0.7756. This weakens the general claim that the diffusion refinement consistently improves the baseline.
We thank the reviewer for this observation. The published LU-Net+RA Dice of 0.7756 was obtained under a different protocol and should not be used to quantify our refinement effect. In our same-image, same-protocol evaluation on the 12 HRF test images, LU-Net+RA achieved a mean Dice of 0.7325. The 20-step and 50-step configurations achieved 0.762 and 0.793, representing absolute increases of 2.95 and 6.05 percentage points, respectively. We will clarify this within-study comparison and retain the published result solely as literature context. These mean scores do not establish statistical significance.

4.	The gain from increasing DDIM steps is relatively small after 50 steps. On CHASE-DB1, Dice increases from 0.835 at 50 steps to only 0.836 at 100 steps, while the reported refinement time doubles from approximately 100 ms to 200 ms. This suggests that 100 steps are computationally inefficient, but the paper should quantify whether the 0.001 difference is simply sampling variation. 
We agree with the reviewer that the Dice increase of 0.001 from 50 to 100 DDIM steps is small relative to the approximately doubled refinement time. We have revised the manuscript to clarify that this difference may reflect sampling variation, although this explanation is not confirmed. The original single-sample results do not establish a statistically reliable advantage for 100 steps. We therefore retain 50 steps as a practical accuracy–cost operating point. Repeated sampling with matched initial-noise seeds on the original checkpoint and test images is required to quantify the difference reliably. 

5.	The 10-to-50 step improvement comes at a substantial computational cost. Dice increases from 0.814 to 0.835 (+0.021), while runtime increases from approximately 20 ms to 100 ms (5×). The paper should report a more formal accuracy–latency analysis rather than describing this only qualitatively. 
We agree with the reviewer and have added a quantitative accuracy–latency analysis. Increasing DDIM steps from 10 to 50 improves Dice by 0.021 (2.1 percentage points), while approximate refinement latency increases from 20 to 100 ms—a fivefold increase—and throughput decreases from 50 to 10 images/s. This corresponds to an incremental gain of 0.002625 Dice per additional 10 ms. We also report the incremental gains between consecutive settings to quantify diminishing returns. We clarify that 50 steps is a practical operating point rather than a demonstrated optimum. These comparisons use approximate refinement-only timings and single-sample Dice estimates; repeated timing and sampling measurements are needed to establish a reliable accuracy–latency frontier.
6.	The effect of DDIM steps is not monotonic across all metrics. On DRIVE, increasing the steps from 20 to 50 raises Dice from 0.795 to 0.835 (+0.040), but sensitivity decreases from 0.835 to 0.825 (-0.010). This indicates that the higher Dice does not correspond to uniformly better vessel detection and warrants analysis of FP/FN changes. We agree that higher Dice does not imply uniformly better vessel detection. We have added a quantitative analysis of the reported error rates. Increasing DDIM steps from 20 to 50 raises Dice by 0.040 but reduces sensitivity by 0.010, while specificity increases from 0.980 to 0.982. Consequently, the derived false-negative rate increases from 0.165 to 0.175, whereas the false-positive rate decreases from 0.020 to 0.018. Under identical evaluation conditions, this is consistent with reduced false-positive rates at the cost of lower vessel recall. We clarify that these rates are calculated from rounded summary metrics and do not constitute measured paired FP/FN counts. Per-image confusion counts and vessel-width analysis remain necessary to quantify absolute error changes and identify the affected vessel structures. The revised manuscript therefore avoids claiming uniformly better detection at 50 steps.
7.	The test sets are very small for drawing strong dataset-level conclusions. CHASE-DB1 has only 8 test images, while HRF has 12. With such small samples, a Dice change of 0.01–0.02 may not be statistically meaningful. Per-image results and 95% confidence intervals should be reported.
Thank you for this comment. We analysed the corrected per-image CHASE-DB1 results using paired subject-level bootstrap resampling, retaining both eyes of each subject together. At 50 DDIM transitions, mean Dice was 0.812244 for the baseline and 0.834999 for refinement. The mean paired increase was 0.022756, with an exploratory 95% bootstrap confidence interval of [0.001686, 0.043826], calculated using 50,000 resamples after averaging 20 sampling realisations per image.
This analysis indicates a positive mean difference in the supplied evaluation table. However, only four independent subjects were available, so the interval should be interpreted cautiously. Improvement was not observed in every image, and these results do not establish broad dataset-level robustness.


8.	The parameter-efficiency claim needs a more informative comparison. The proposed refinement module adds 502,401 parameters, but the manuscript does not provide a directly comparable parameter count for the LU-Net+RA baseline using the same counting convention. Therefore, it is difficult to judge how lightweight the complete system actually is.
We agree and have added a directly comparable parameter-count table using the same convention: all unique weights and biases are counted, including frozen parameters. At the same time, buffers and optimizer states are excluded. LU-Net+RA contains 1,946,961 parameters. Our audit identified that the previously reported 502,401-parameter subtotal omitted a separate conditioning MLP. Including this layer gives 535,425 refinement parameters and 2,482,386 parameters for the complete archived implementation, representing 27.50% overhead. We separately report the new reproduction, which contains 527,233 refinement parameters and 2,474,194 complete-system parameters (27.08% overhead). The revised manuscript distinguishes total parameters from those updated during refinement training and clarifies that parameter counts alone do not establish computational efficiency. We verified the archived architecture against its saved model state, but we cannot verify its association with the deleted original experimental run.

9.	The 128-dimensional global image embedding may be too restrictive for thin-vessel refinement. The diffusion model receives only a 128-dimensional global vector from the RGB image rather than spatial image features. Since the main motivation is recovery of local thin vessels and boundaries, an ablation comparing global conditioning with spatial conditioning would be useful.
Thank you for this comment. We have added a separate matched ablation comparing global and spatial image conditioning across all three datasets, using five independently trained refiners per configuration. The conditioning variants have matched parameter counts, although their computational costs differ.



10.	The latent representation is not actually compressed in terms of scalar count. The input mask contains 262,144 values, whereas the encoded representation contains 1,048,576 values (4× more). Therefore, calling this a lightweight latent-space representation is misleading; it is spatially downsampled but has a larger total feature count.
We agree with the reviewer. The input mask contains 512 × 512 = 262,144 scalar values, while the encoded feature grid contains 64 × 128 × 128 = 1,048,576 scalar values—four times as many. Thus, the representation is spatially downsampled by a factor of four along each axis, but it is not compressed in terms of total scalar count. The manuscript explicitly distinguishes these concepts and describes the representation as a spatially downsampled feature grid. Parameter efficiency refers to the model’s parameter budget, rather than compression of the encoded mask.


11.	The contribution of the Dice term is not demonstrated. The diffusion objective uses λDice = 0.5, but there is no ablation with λDice = 0 or alternative weights. Since this term directly introduces segmentation supervision, its contribution should be separated from the diffusion denoising objective.
Thank you for this comment. We have added a Dice-weight ablation on CHASE-DB1 using λDice = 0, 0.25, 0.5, and 1, with three training seeds and five sampling seeds per trained model at 50 DDIM transitions. The corresponding mean Dice scores were 0.834079, 0.834343, 0.834988, and 0.835943.
Compared with the denoising-only refiner objective (λDice = 0), λDice = 0.5 increased mean Dice by 0.000910. The exploratory paired t-based 95% interval across the three training-seed means was [0.000827, 0.000992]. This interval describes training-seed variability on the fixed test set and does not account for uncertainty across images or subjects.
These results indicate a small observed contribution from the additional Dice supervision. However, λDice = 1 achieved the highest mean, so we do not claim that 0.5 is optimal. Removing the Dice term isolates its direct contribution to refiner training while retaining the previously supervised backbone and autoencoder. We added the ablation tables, analysis, and associated limitations to the manuscript.

12.	The claimed inference throughput is incomplete. The reported 10 images/s at 50 transitions excludes the LU-Net+RA forward pass. Therefore, this is not the throughput of the complete proposed segmentation pipeline. The full end-to-end latency should be reported.


Thank you for pointing out this omission. We have clarified that the original estimate of approximately 10 images/s describes refinement-stage throughput only and added a complete-pipeline benchmark.
On an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti SUPER, using FP32, batch size one, and 50 DDIM transitions, mean latency was 119.02 ms for LU-Net+RA followed by refinement. Including preprocessing, input transfers, thresholding, and returning the segmentation mask to CPU increased mean latency to 140.50 ms, corresponding to approximately 7.12 images/s for serial execution. This measurement starts from a decoded RGB image in CPU memory and excludes disk access, JPEG decoding, model loading, and output-file saving.
Each timing mode used five warm-up runs and ten CUDA-synchronised measurements per image across eight images. The manuscript now reports timing scope and dispersion explicitly. These measurements use the available reproduction checkpoints and are distinguished from the original timing estimates.

13.	The repeated-sampling experiment is quantitatively underdeveloped. Variance is shown for one CHASE-DB1 image, but the manuscript does not provide the number of samples, nor a quantitative relationship between variance and segmentation error. Reporting the variance–error correlation over the complete test set would make this analysis substantially more meaningful.
Thank you for this comment. We agree that the single-image variance visualisation does not establish a quantitative relationship between sampling variability and segmentation error. The supplied evaluation table contains 20 sampling realisations per image for all eight CHASE-DB1 test images. However, these image-level Dice scores and confusion counts are insufficient to reconstruct pixel-wise probability variance or calculate its correlation with segmentation error. This analysis requires the corresponding repeated probability maps. Therefore, we do not claim that the displayed variance is calibrated uncertainty or a validated predictor of segmentation error. A complete variance–error analysis remains necessary.





14.	The absence of augmentation is potentially important given the small datasets. The model uses no image, geometric, or mask augmentation, despite training on only 20 CHASE-DB1 and 20 DRIVE images (and 33 HRF images). The authors should either justify this choice or provide an augmentation ablation.

Thank you for highlighting this issue. The diffusion refiner was trained without additional image, geometric, or mask augmentation, using fixed preprocessing and stored coarse predictions. This setting evaluates refinement under a fixed input distribution, but it does not establish that augmentation is unnecessary or that the chosen training strategy is optimal.
We agree that the small training sets make this limitation important. An augmentation ablation would require consistent transformations of the RGB image, coarse probability map, and reference mask to preserve their alignment. Since this comparison has not been performed, we cannot quantify the effect of augmentation or claim robustness to such transformations.
